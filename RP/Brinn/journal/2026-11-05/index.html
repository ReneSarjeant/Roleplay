Adventures in Elwynn Forest

Goldshire, Elwynn Forest

5 November, Year 25 — Part One

Brinn left the abbey with yesterday’s lessons still fresh in her hands. The shelves had offered a welcome sort of order: books returned to their places, names attached to things, a task whose end could be seen. Northshire’s other troubles were less obliging.

The Defias were still there.

“This land belongs to the Defias Brotherhood now!” one of the men shouted.

Brinn had heard enough people announce that something belonged to them. It rarely improved matters for the people already living there.

She fought her way through the remaining work and carried the recovered red bandanas back to Deputy Willem. He had more names for her: Milly Osworth, whose harvest needed recovering, and Garrick Padfoot, whose name appeared on a bounty. Brinn listened, asked what was needed, and found her morning growing longer before she had properly begun it.

She spoke with Milly and took on the task of recovering her harvest. At Echo Ridge, she finished the skirmish Marshal McBride had sent her to undertake. When she returned to the marshal, he had work beyond the valley for her.

Goldshire.

Brinn tucked his documents safely away. A proper road, a village, an inn. Somewhere she might learn the southern countryside by more than the things trying to bite her in it.

“You are dismissed, Brinn,” McBride said.

She stepped out beneath the trees and drew a long breath.

There was still plenty she had agreed to do. For the moment, though, there was a road before her, and she was allowed to follow it.

Goldshire announced itself in voices, footsteps, and the steady business of people trying to get through a day.

Brinn liked it before she had decided whether that was sensible.

Near the village, Sam Sarsaparilla invited her to sit beside his campfire. For once, someone’s first instruction was to rest.

She obeyed with very little argument.

Sam explained how a basic fire could become the heart of a proper camp, with each traveler’s trade contributing something useful. A tent, a fixture, something to make the next stretch of road easier. Brinn listened closely when his lessons led toward cooking and herbalism.

That made sense to her. A company did not remain standing because one person knew everything. Someone tended the fire. Someone knew what could be eaten. Someone kept the rain off the blankets.

The flames settled into a comfortable glow.

“Every scar’s a lesson,” she said, grinning into them. “I’m very well educated.”

She stayed long enough to feel the benefit of sitting still, then delivered McBride’s papers to Marshal Dughan.

Goldshire, it appeared, had its own list of troubles.

There was the Fargodeep Mine to investigate. Remy wanted gold dust. William Pestle wanted candles. Brinn began to suspect that an introduction to any village in Elwynn consisted chiefly of being told what had gone missing beneath it.

Inside the Lion’s Pride Inn, Farley offered a more welcoming introduction.

“Welcome to the Lion’s Pride Inn. Make yourself at home!”

She found a place to rest and let the room carry on around her. The abbey had given her work and shelter. This felt different: a place she might return to, with roads leading out in several directions and ordinary lives carrying on outside its door.

Goldshire, then.

For now, it would do very nicely.

Brinn had scarcely settled herself in Goldshire before someone offered her another reason to stay.

The invitation bore a curious name.

Mid Life Aggro.

She read it twice.

“Well,” she murmured, “at least they’re honest about having troubles.”

Around her, the village carried on. Someone was working linen into useful things; somewhere nearby, meat hissed over a cooking fire. Travelers compared their next errands while others stood quite still, enjoying the rare luxury of having nowhere urgent to be.

It reminded her, a little, of the better evenings on the road. Those brief hours when the wagons stopped and people became people again, instead of another column trying to reach somewhere safer.

In Brinn’s telling of it afterward, Saeloren’s invitation came with a simple explanation: there were plenty of roads ahead, and no particular virtue in walking every one alone.

She hesitated.

“I’m a sister,” she said. “I can tend a wound. Still learning what to do about the thing that caused it.”

Apparently, that was no obstacle.

She glanced toward the road out of Goldshire. There were mines beneath those hills, creatures in the woods, and a growing collection of promises tucked into her bag. She had already discovered how quickly being useful could become being in trouble.

Some company might be welcome.

“All right, then,” she said. “Provided nobody expects a sermon before breakfast.”

She accepted.

For a moment, she considered making a proper introduction. Her name, perhaps, and where she had come from. Something about her work.

But the greetings were already passing back and forth, easy and unceremonious.

“Morning,” Brinn offered.

That would do. There would be time for the rest.

She adjusted her bag and found herself smiling as she stepped back into the village. Goldshire was beginning to feel less like somewhere she had been sent and more like somewhere she knew people.

Later, when Du Track turned her twenty-eight pieces of linen into four splendid bags, she would have further cause to approve of her decision.

For now, it was enough to know that someone might answer when she called.

Priestess Anetta’s referral brought her to Priestess Josetta, and Josetta’s task brought her to Guard Roberts.

Here, at last, was something Brinn understood without first having to be persuaded of it.

A wounded man. A prayer. The next useful thing.

She knelt within reach and gathered the Light with the care Maud had taught her to give every measure and every strip of linen. Her instruction in Lesser Heal had a purpose beyond keeping herself upright in the woods. It belonged here, too, with someone who needed help and could not simply leave his post to find it.

“Ah, priestess, you came along just in time. I appreciate it.”

She watched him recover, waiting until she was satisfied before drawing back.

“Thank you! Thank you, priestess. Now I can take on those murlocs with the Light on my side!”

That earned him a small smile.

“Mind you keep yourself in one piece, then.”

His farewell followed her: “Farewell to you, and may the Light be with you always.”

For a little while afterward, the morning seemed very straightforward.

She completed Josetta’s task and continued her training, learning to shape a protective shield and strengthen her Smite. There was comfort in that work. Protection before injury. Healing afterward. A clear reason for each.

She wished every lesson stayed so clear.

Stormwind rose above the road with enough stone to make a traveler feel briefly unfinished.

Brinn slowed on the great approach. Water lay beneath the bridge, and five immense figures watched over the way into the city.

She studied the names: Turalyon, Khadgar, Alleria Windrunner, Danath Trollbane, Kurdran Wildhammer. The leaders of the expedition beyond the Dark Portal, honored here for the sacrifice made far from home.

Soldiers passed below those stone faces carrying parcels, messages, and the untidy business of being alive. Brinn stood aside to let them through.

She had known people who never came home. None had received a statue.

For a moment she thought of Ham, then let the thought rest where she could bear it. These were other people’s names, other people’s missing. She would not borrow their ending for his.

Inside the gates, the city quickly became less solemn. Bread sellers called over the traffic. Errands led around the canals. A wine-shop advertisement became a visit to the Gallina Winery, and Brinn found time for instruction in Enchanting as well.

There were more ways to make a useful thing than she had learned on the refugee roads. She was curious enough to see what this one might offer.

Then Elwynn called her back out beneath its trees.

The Stonefields and Maclures had managed to build a considerable amount of trouble out of neighboring farms.

Maybell had a letter for Tommy Joe. Tommy Joe needed help. Gramma Stonefield had advice and a note for William Pestle. Auntie Bernice had lost a necklace, and Billy Maclure possessed information he was in no hurry to part with.

He wanted pie.

Brinn considered the boy, the missing necklace, and the distance back to the other farm.

“A pie,” she repeated. “Well, you’ve thought that through.”

Four pieces of boar meat later, she was beginning to appreciate just how thoroughly he had thought it through.

She carried the meat to Bernice, then carried the finished pork belly pie back to Billy. The promised information finally led her toward Goldtooth. The necklace itself remained another piece of unfinished business.

There was also a troublesome pig called Princess, whose name seemed to have done little for her manners.

Between farms, woods, and the Fargodeep Mine, Brinn learned that the pleasant green of Elwynn concealed plenty of teeth. She fought wolves, spiders, boars, and kobolds. Some encounters went badly enough to strip the confidence from her earlier laughter.

And somewhere among those fights, another habit began.

Shadow Word: Pain.

At Northshire it had been a spell she knew. Now she was using it against every creature she fought.

The recognition troubled her more than any single casting had.

She could explain each encounter. Something had attacked. Something else had been near enough to join it. She needed time to strike again, time to finish the fight, time to remain alive. In the moment, the word came readily.

Too readily, perhaps.

She still prayed to the Light. She still wanted wounded people to stand again, and Guard Roberts’s gratitude had mattered more to her than a pocketful of gold dust ever could. Yet she could no longer tell herself that learning the darker spell had left it untouched on a shelf.

She had begun reaching for it.

Brinn stood for a moment outside Goldshire and let that truth settle without dressing it up.

She would have to watch herself. That was a smaller promise than claiming she understood it, and one she might actually keep.

Remy’s warning about murlocs sent her back to Marshal Dughan, and Dughan’s concerns carried her farther along the road to Guard Thomas.

Near Crystal Lake, there were fishing traps to inspect and shiny objects to recover from a murloc camp. William Pestle needed crystal kelp for the lovers’ predicament. Brinn accepted the work without pretending that accepting it had finished it.

Her bags were becoming a practical objection to all this helpfulness.

Linen, supplies, things required by one person or another: every small item seemed to arrive with an excellent reason to keep it. The spaces available to carry them had no interest in those reasons.

By the time she returned to Stormwind, she was level seven, travel-worn, and very ready for one problem to become easier.

Then Du Track, a fellow member of Mid Life Aggro, met her for the linen.

Twenty-eight pieces of cloth.

Four linen bags in return.

Brinn watched the work with an attention that would have suited a much grander ceremony. A seam drawn tight. Another useful pocket. Another bag taking shape where there had only been folded cloth.

Four of them.

She could put things away without first conducting an argument with everything she already owned.

She was so pleased that a sensible thank-you proved entirely insufficient. There was a hug, a blown kiss, and laughter she made no attempt to swallow. For once, she was simply twenty years old and delighted.

“We did it!” Du Track said.

“We made it,” Brinn answered.

They exchanged cheers. He saluted; she waved him off, still smiling.

She checked the bags again afterward.

They were still four bags.

That remained marvelous.

With her belongings finally given room to breathe, Brinn found her way to the Gilded Rose in Stormwind’s Trade District. The road could wait while she sat, put down her newly manageable burdens, and let the morning catch up with her.

She settled into a chair and rested a hand on the nearest bag.

“Right, then. The kingdom can manage without me for one cup. I’ve finally somewhere to put my things.”

5 November, Year 25 — Part Two

The four bags were still marvelous when Brinn left Stormwind. They were also beginning to fill.

Crystal Lake had seen to that. Jason Mathers’s fishing traps needed checking, the murlocs needed driving back, and Lee Brown wanted the shiny rubbish they had carried off. Brinn returned to the shore with room for everything except another set of teeth.

The lake supplied those freely.

It was unpleasant work, and dangerous enough to punish her whenever she grew careless. Between the mud, the sudden rushes from the water, and the distance back to safety, she found little of the peaceful afternoon she had imagined over her cup.

Lee Brown could teach her to fish. That ought to have been encouraging.

She counted her money instead.

One silver stood between her and a quiet stretch of bank. For the moment, she needed what she had for the less restful business of staying alive.

Later, in Goldshire, she heard Oona Hexspark remark, “There is good fish to be found in the waters of Elwynn, that is true.”

“The fishing trainer wanted 1 silver. I have to wait to fish,” Brinn answered.

Saying it aloud made it sound rather more pitiful than she had intended.

Still, there were jobs to finish. She returned to them.

William Pestle took the crystal kelp and set to work.

“This shouldn’t take long…”

Brinn watched with professional interest. A measure, a mixture, a purpose stranger than any wash Maud had taught her. The lovers at the neighboring farms had somehow brought her from a missing necklace and a pork pie to invisibility liquor.

“The invisibility liquor is ready for you, Brinn.”

She carried it to Maybell Maclure with considerably more care than she had carried Billy’s pie.

“Here goes nothing…” Maybell said.

Brinn hoped it would be enough. She had done the work asked of her; whatever came next would belong to Maybell and Tommy Joe.

Back in Goldshire, Priestess Josetta’s instruction gave her something steadier to consider. Renew offered another way to tend an injury, its healing continuing after the first prayer. Fade was a different sort of lesson: sometimes surviving meant drawing less attention to oneself.

She could see a use for that.

The Fargodeep Mine had already given her reasons.

Goldshire kept collecting people around its fires.

Earlier, one had burned an extraordinary purple. Brinn stopped with the others to look at it.

“Nice fire,” she said, before considering the matter more carefully.

“The purple flames are the work of warlock I imagine.”

It was a guess. She had no intention of putting a hand into it to improve the answer.

Later, at a campfire in the village, the talk turned to weather. People settled nearby, exchanged remarks, and enjoyed a few minutes in which nothing was trying to bite anyone.

Brinn contributed a story about outrunning half the Stormwind Guard on a dare, only to be caught by the other half. Told beside a fire, it had the sound of a good tale rather than a statement anyone ought to take down for the watch.

“No rain, it is a nice day today,” she said.

Talon Hawklight admitted a liking for thunderstorms. Keredria Oakheart wondered whether they were beautiful; Arn Bridgeway called the day peaceful.

Brinn sat a little longer than she needed to.

There were worse uses for an afternoon than listening to people talk about the sky.

Eventually, Jason’s murloc troubles led through Remy Two Times to a scheme involving animal blood and duskweed petals for bait. Brinn gathered what was needed, carried the results back, and finally returned to Lee Brown with enough coin to become his student.

Fishing could happen after all.

She tried not to look quite as pleased as she had over the bags.

She was getting a great deal of practice at failing that.

Fargodeep still had unfinished business waiting in the dark.

Brinn went back for the necklace, the gold dust, and the candles. The kobolds objected with their usual lack of ambiguity. She emerged with Bernice’s necklace at last, then made the rounds of the people whose errands had become the contents of her bags.

One thing returned. Another delivered. A little space recovered.

William’s shipment took her into Stormwind again. This time, after finding Morgan Pestle, she allowed herself an errand of her own.

A fishing pole.

By the water, two boys were conducting a loud and remarkably productive discussion about fish neither appeared to have caught.

“I heard that there are these huge fish that can walk on land to hunt, and eat people!” Billy announced.

After Crystal Lake, Brinn thought that deserved a more serious hearing than it received.

Adam was unimpressed.

“If you fished as well as you talked the ocean wouldn’t have any fish left.”

Brinn lowered her line and kept her amusement to herself.

There were smallfish in the water. Mud snappers, too. There were also stretches when nothing happened, and she discovered how much she had wanted exactly that.

Her hands had a task. Her eyes had somewhere to rest. She could wait without wondering whether someone would die because she had taken too long.

When a fish finally took the bait, she brought it in with an attention usually reserved for work much more urgent.

“There you are.”

She stayed for another cast.

The practice later earned Lee Brown’s approval and instruction in preparing a Fish Bowl for camp. For once, an afternoon’s work had begun with a quiet hope and ended with something she could put in a bowl.

The road east brought her back to danger.

Michelle Belle’s First Aid lessons gave practical form to the cloth work Brinn already knew. Herbalist Pomeroy taught her the preparation of an incense candle, and she began formal study of Alchemy as well.

Names, measures, clean materials. She welcomed the familiar discipline.

Jasperlode Mine was less welcoming. Hamish Bergwort at the Tower of Azora needed kobold geomancers dealt with; Marshal Dughan needed to know what was happening underground. Brinn did the work and returned with her report, although another task for Hamish remained ahead of her.

Near Princess, she found a welcome chance to share a fight.

“Princess?” Morrigan Emberblast asked, the meaning clear enough.

Brinn had scarcely begun asking for help herself.

“Yes.”

The mage invited her to join forces. Together they dealt with the troublesome boar, and Brinn recovered the brass collar she needed.

“Thanks,” she said.

It was a brief partnership. It made the work easier all the same.

Farther east, the requests multiplied. Tools had washed downstream from Eastvale Logging Camp. Sara Timberlain wanted red linen bandanas recovered from the Defias. Supervisor Raelen needed bundles of wood gathered from the forest.

Guard Thomas had a different concern.

Two guards had not come back.

Their names were Rolf and Malakai.

Brinn followed the search north along the river. By then she had spent enough of the day fighting murlocs to recognize the danger in the places where they gathered. She went carefully.

Care could not change what she found.

The search became a search for Rolf’s fate, and then there were two medallions to carry back to Thomas.

She held them a moment before putting them away.

Names mattered. A guard waiting at a bridge needed to know what had happened to the men he had sent out. Someone would have to tell whoever waited for them elsewhere.

Brinn could bring back the answer. She wished she had been able to bring back the men.

There was no useful prayer that would make her arrive earlier.

She returned to Thomas, delivered the medallions, and took his report to Marshal Dughan. The road between those errands felt longer than it had before.

She finished the work around Eastvale, carried the wood and bandanas back, and helped make the frontier safer from the prowlers and young bears threatening it. Bernice received Princess’s collar. Sara received the armor marker Dughan had entrusted to Brinn.

The errands were getting done.

That ought to have felt better than it did.

Back in Goldshire, Michelle’s further instruction gave Brinn a First Aid Kit to work toward, and she learned the preparation of a Minor Healing Potion through that same practical study.

Josetta had more to teach as well.

A stronger Lesser Heal. Resurrection.

Brinn gave that lesson her full attention. After the river, she could hardly have done otherwise. She listened carefully to what was taught, without allowing herself to turn a new prayer into a promise it had not made.

There were darker lessons beside it: Mind Blast, and a stronger form of Shadow Word: Pain.

She learned those too.

That was the uncomfortable part. She could still feel the relief of having another means of defending herself, even while remembering how much of the day she had spent hurting things.

Knowing another spell did not settle when she ought to use it.

She would have liked someone to teach that part as plainly.

Josetta sent her to High Priestess Laurena in Stormwind. Brinn would go. First, she needed to sit down.

At the Gilded Rose, she finally put her bags beside a chair and let her shoulders drop.

She had come to Northshire offering to heal people.

Since then there had been wolves, kobolds, spiders, murlocs, Defias, and a pig with a royal name. There were sensible reasons for the work. Brinn knew them. She was tired of carrying those reasons alongside everything else.

“I’m a healer,” she said quietly.

The words brought no answer from the room. She had needed to hear them anyway.

Rain caught Stormwind before she finished her business.

It came down heavily enough to blur the far side of the street. Brinn watched it for a moment, remembering the pleasant discussion beside the Goldshire fire.

She hoped it was dry there.

At the Cathedral of Light, she found High Priestess Laurena and completed Josetta’s referral, learning Divine Grace. Beneath the cathedral’s shelter, she could give her attention to the lesson while the city dripped outside.

Her faith was still there. It had been there beside Roberts in the morning and beside her fishing line in the afternoon. It had been there when she carried the medallions back.

She did not need to manufacture a grand declaration about it. She needed to keep doing the work with care.

Then she stepped out into the rain again.

It followed her all the way to Goldshire.

Water gathered in her loose auburn hair and found its way beneath her collar. The road shone. Her robe grew heavy at the hem. By the time the Lion’s Pride appeared ahead, she had developed several firm opinions about thunderstorms, none of them especially poetic.

Inside, Tomas taught her the beginnings of Cooking: plain meat, eggs, and a basic campfire. It was modest work to finish a long day, and she was glad of it. There would be use for that knowledge on the road.

When she came in, the rain finally ended.

Brinn looked toward the doorway, then down at her wet sleeves.

“You might have managed that ten minutes sooner.”

She found a place to settle, with four bags beside her and a fishing pole among her belongings. Several errands remained. They could remain until morning.

For tonight, she was back in Goldshire.

She had learned enough new ways to fight for one day. Tomorrow, she hoped, would give her more chances to heal.
