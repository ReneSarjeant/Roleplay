# Sister René Sarjeant

Human priest of the Holy Light. Twenty years old in year 25. Born in a dyer's cottage in the outer wards of Lordaeron's capital, in the last years of Terenas. Fifteen when the bells went wrong. When the work is done for the day, she likes to fish.

## From the Yard

The birth was ordinary. A neighbor held the lamp. Her mother, Edith, kept the yard and the bitter leaves the wash-women used on cloth. Her father, Ham, kept the vats and the door. They named her René because it was short enough to call across a lane, and Sarjeant because that was the name on the cottage.

She was a winter child. The first thing the ward agreed on was that she had her mother's eyes, grey-green, and her father's silence. She learned the yard before she learned the street. Edith put a leaf in her hand and made her say if it had turned. Ham let her stand on a stool at the vat and would not let her lean. The Second War was over before she could walk. What she got of it was a story at the table, a missing uncle, and a rule: do not open the door to a voice you cannot put a face on.

By eight she could name the kitchen herbs and the leaves that were not for eating. By ten she was carrying water and helping with the bitter leaves. The city, from the outer wards, was bells, wash-lines, and the smell of the vats. She saw the palace once, from a rise, on a feast day. She does not remember the throne room. She remembers her father saying they had work at home.

She was fifteen when the bells went wrong.

They had been told the prince was coming home and the gates would hold. Edith was in the yard with the knife she used on the bitter leaves. Ham was on the step. René was between them, a coil of undyed linen in her arms because she had been told to bring it in before the weather turned. The smell arrived before the noise: sweet, then burnt, then nothing a wash was mixed for. Someone shouted that the prince was inside the walls.

Ham looked at the lane, then at René, then at the door. He took the linen out of her arms and put his hand on the back of her coat.

"Go," he said.

That is the word she has. She does not have another.

Edith took the collar and ran. They made the gate in a press of people who were not soldiers. René looked back once. The cottage door was shut. Her father had stayed to bar it. She has been told since that a barred door did not matter by then. She was fifteen. She did not have that sentence yet.

## The Roads

Southshore sorted the living. A clerk wrote Sarjeant the way Edith said it. They slept in a shed that had been a net loft. René learned the new rules without a lesson: do not ask who is coming up the road until you can see their face, do not eat a loaf you cannot name, do not tell the stair to anyone who wants it for a speech. Edith told it once, to a man in a scarlet tabard who called the city already lost and the people in it already judged. Edith said her husband was not a sermon. They left before the week was out.

The roads south took what the city had not. René grew on them. She turned sixteen in a ditch while a column waited out a rain. She kept the habit of the yard. Verges, hedgerows, the leaves that had turned. She tore linen the way Ham had folded it, clean side in.

## Maud

A field sister joined the column when René was sixteen and stayed because the column kept producing wounded. Her name was Maud. She was not young, and she was not kind for show. She watched René sort leaves for two days and then said the girl could carry the bag if she could follow a rule.

Maud taught three books and did not pretend they were one.

The ground: what a verge will allow, what has turned, what will poison a wash if it is cut too late. René had the yard already. Maud made her say the name before she cut.

The still: measures, labels, the wash that closes a shallow burn, the draught that cuts a fever if the fever is the kind a draught can cut. A jar labeled twice was a jar someone had checked. René liked that. She still does.

The cloth: linen torn to width, folded, packed so the clean side stayed clean. Maud said a flask and a roll were different work, and that a girl who mixed them would kill someone by being proud of the wrong tool. The still does not bind a wound. The roll does not cut a fever.

The Light was not offered as a fourth book. Maud prayed the way she labeled: short, specific, said aloud so the person on the ground could hear the step they were on. She said the Light was not impressed by a jar and not offended by one either. René disliked the sentence. It sounded like a door left open on purpose. She disliked it until a driver stopped bleeding where the draught had already done all it could, and the bandage was the rest, and the prayer was only the name of the next thing to do.

Maud died when René was eighteen, of a fever the draught was not mixed for. René sat with her because that was the work. The wounded had already been calling her sister. She took the title the week after, not from a bishop. No one on a cot had asked what a refugee girl was supposed to believe. She kept the collar. She did not put the still down. She did not put the roll down.

## The Empty Jar

The empty jar is from the first landing she worked alone, the winter she was nineteen. A company had a man burned by a spilled pot. She mixed the wash to Maud's measure. It beaded on the skin and did nothing. The burn was past it. She put her hands there because the flask was empty and the roll was already on the worse of the other two, and the burn closed halfway, bright and wrong and in no recipe she owned. She got him to a cot. She labeled the jar in her own hand and sealed it empty. She has not refilled it. It is the measure that was not enough.

She has been offered the other road twice. A preacher, when she was nineteen, said Lordaeron could be scoured clean if the faithful were willing to be terrible. A knight said the same thing with better armor. She told the first that her father was not a stain to be burnt out of a verse. She told the second she would bind his men if they were bleeding and would not march under his banner. She has not been invited a third time.

Edith took a different road when René was nineteen. A letter from a chapel kitchen in Stormwind said she was well, that the work was plain, and that René should not come unless the company was stood down. René keeps the letter in the same pocket as the jar. She has not gone to the kitchen.

She is twenty now. The southern ground is a different book from the Lordaeron yard, and she is learning it on her own. She no longer travels with a company. She walks Azeroth with the still and the roll packed, taking the work that needs a sister who brews, binds, and does not preach a quota. She sits with a soldier who wants a psalm, a cook who wants the proportion of a salve, or a traveler who wants neither.

She will not say Undercity. Home, as it was, and then she does not say it again. She has not decided what she will do if the door opens and something answers to his name.

The still is packed. The roll is packed beside it. The jar is empty. That is the part that hurts. When the day is done, she likes to fish.

## Maud's Influence

Maud did not save René. Edith had already done that, with a hand on a collar and a gate. What Maud did was decide the girl was old enough to be useful, and then refuse to let useful become sentimental.

She joined the column when René was sixteen. She watched for two days before she spoke. René was sorting verge-leaf the way the yard had taught her, by look, not by name. Maud said she could carry the bag if she could follow a rule. The rule was not obedience. It was: say what the thing is before you use it. A leaf. A measure. A strip of cloth. A prayer. Maud had no patience for a hand that moved faster than its mouth.

That is the whole of the influence, under the stories. René still works that way. She labels aloud. She prays aloud. She tells a recruit the next step while she is doing it, not after, because Maud said a person on the ground cannot follow a silence. The spare voice is not a personality René invented. It is the last teaching method she trusted.

The three books are Maud's, and René has not folded them. Herbalism is the ground. Alchemy is the still. First Aid is the cloth. Maud split them because a proud girl will reach for the tool she is best at, and the wound does not care what she is best at. René was best at the yard. Maud made her tear linen until the roll was as quick as the knife. On the road now, the flask and the bandage live in different parts of the bag. That is not flavor. That is Maud standing in the packing.

The Light is the part René did not inherit cleanly. Maud never offered it as a craft. She prayed the way she labeled: short, specific, no claim that the asking had earned the answer. The sentence René disliked — the Light is not impressed by a jar and not offended by one either — was Maud refusing both the preacher and the tinker. A scouring faith would have used René's father as fuel. A faith that bowed to the still would have let René hide in measures. Maud left her between them, which is why René can sit with a psalm, a proportion, or neither, and why she has no sermon quota. She was not trained to perform belief. She was trained not to lie about what closed the wound.

What René took without being told is harder. Maud did not leave. Columns move. Maud stayed where the wounded were. René calls that the work. It is also a pattern. She used to take companies. She does not take kitchens. Edith's letter asked her not to come unless the company was stood down, and René has obeyed it with relief she does not examine. Maud gave her a way to love people without keeping their road. That is a gift. It is also how you fail to go see your mother.

The death is the lesson René will not sand down. Maud died of a fever the draught was not mixed for. René had the bag, the measures, the names. None of them were the right book. She sat anyway, because Maud had sat. The title came the week after, from the wounded, not from a bishop. René wears it as a work smock because that is how Maud wore hers.

The empty jar is where Maud's teaching breaks, and where René starts. She mixed to the measure she was given. The measure was not enough. The burn closed halfway under her hands, in no recipe Maud wrote. A faithful copy would have called it a failure and stopped. René labeled the empty jar and kept it. She did not tell herself the Light had been impressed. She did not throw out the still. She carries the proof that the woman who taught her was right about tools and silent about the thing that is not a tool.

Maud's limit is that she finished. René has not. Maud could say the Light was not offended by a jar because Maud was not going back to a door. René is. If she becomes only Maud with a younger face, she will bind the wounded, refuse the scarlet banner, and never decide what the stair was for. The influence holds in the bag. It does not hold at the gate. That part is René's, and Maud is not there to name it before she uses it.

## The Light

The Church René inherited is not a god with a face. In the Lordaeron teaching, the Holy Light is a power that answers faith, will, and the ask made for someone else's good. Archbishop Faol's order put that into three virtues, and the roadside version is plainer than the cathedral one.

Respect: the person on the cot is not a means. Tenacity: you stay when the work is not finished. Compassion: you spend the draught, the cloth, and the prayer on the one in front of you, not on the idea of them. Uther's knights were taught the same three, then handed a hammer. Maud was taught them and handed a bag. René got the bag.

Maud's correction is the sentence René disliked. The Light is not impressed by a jar and not offended by one either. That is a refusal of two heresies the refugee roads were selling.

The first heresy is payment. Mix it right, pray it right, suffer enough, and the Light owes you the city. Maud would not say that. A fever took her that the draught was not mixed for. If the Light kept accounts, Maud died in credit, and the accounts are a lie. René will not pray "give me the kingdom back." That ask treats the Light as a clerk who lost a file.

The second heresy is purity. The scarlet road says the Light is offended by the unclean, and that offense is a license to burn. René heard it at Southshore when she was fifteen, in a man's mouth, aimed at her father. She heard it again at nineteen. She will not march under it. Respect fails the moment Ham Sarjeant becomes a stain in someone else's verse. Compassion fails the moment the cot is sorted into worth saving and worth scouring. Tenacity without the other two is just staying for the killing.

What she holds is smaller. The Light is not a recipe, which is why she cannot write a proportion for it. A herb fails out of its ground. A flask fails when the wound is past it. A roll fails when the cloth runs out. Those fail honestly. A prayer fails when she lies about what she is asking. What she can ask is the next thing: hold this edge, let this man see morning, do not make me reach for the wrong book. She says it aloud so the ally can hear the step. Maud prayed like a label. René kept the method.

The empty jar is the doctrine she did not inherit. She mixed to the measure. The measure was not enough. The burn closed halfway under her hands, in no page Maud left. She did not call it earned. She did not call the Light impressed. She sealed the jar empty so she would not start trusting a clean label more than the wound. If she ever makes a theology of that hour, it will be this: the Light is not the still, and it is not ashamed of the still. It arrived where the tool ended. It did not arrive to congratulate her.

She does not know, and will not pretend to know, what that means for the door. The risen of Lordaeron are the wound in the teaching. Faol's knights were told the Light keeps faith with the living. The city is full of the dead who walk. A scarlet answer is that the Light has already judged them. René does not have that answer, and she does not have the other one either, the soft one that says every name behind the gate is only waiting to be called home. She was fifteen. She has a word, "go," and a shut door. Hope that writes her father's ending for him is the same sin as the scouring, done in a kinder voice.

So the working creed, if a confessor asked, would be short enough to pack.

The Light is not a wage. The three virtues are the test of an ask, not a spell component. Respect, tenacity, compassion, in that order when they conflict. Tools are allowed. Tools are not owed an answer. She will bind a scarlet knight who is bleeding and will not carry his banner. She will not say Undercity. She will not decide on paper what she does if something answers to Ham's name. What holds is the next person on the cot. The rest is above her measure, and she has already kept one jar to prove she knows the difference.

## The Second Book


She did not set the Light down. She still packs the still and the roll. She still says the name before she cuts. The empty jar is still empty.


What changed was the road past the last company.


Maud’s three books covered the ground, the draught, and the cloth. None of them answered what lived in the places the Light did not reach cleanly — the lingering cold under a ruin, the thing that kept walking after the prayer had already done its work, the stretch of night between one safe fire and the next when she was alone and the mana was low. The Light closed the wound. It did not always stop the thing that had made the wound. She had already learned, at nineteen, that a measure could be exact and still not enough.


Shadow was not offered as a fourth book and not taken as a rejection of the first three. It was the one that let her keep walking. A killing blow that returned breath instead of demanding another drink. A drain that kept a pack off her back when she could not outrun it. A veil that let her stand in the dark places long enough to see whether the Light was even being asked for. She treats it the way Maud treated the still and the roll: separate, labeled, used for what it is for. She does not preach it. She does not call it the answer. She calls it the tool that keeps the still packed and the roll dry when the road does not end at a chapel.


She still wears the Friar’s Robes. She still will not say Undercity. When the day is done she still likes to fish. The second book is simply open now, because the world past the last company is wider than the first three books, and she has not yet decided she is finished walking it.
